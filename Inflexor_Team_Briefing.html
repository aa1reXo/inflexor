Fluency Check

VC 101 — Terms You Should Be Able to Use Cold

Basic vocabulary so a casual reference to any of these doesn't stall the conversation.

Term Sheet

A non-binding document outlining the key terms of a proposed investment (valuation, amount, rights) before the full legal agreements are drafted. Signing one signals serious intent, not a done deal.

SHA (Shareholders' Agreement)

The binding legal contract between the company and its shareholders (including the new investor) once a round closes — governs voting rights, board composition, exit rights, and investor protections.

Cap Table

Short for "capitalization table" — a ledger of who owns what percentage of a company, across founders, employees (via ESOP), and every investor round.

Pre-money / Post-money Valuation

Pre-money = the company's agreed value before new investment is added. Post-money = pre-money + the new investment amount. The investor's ownership % = investment ÷ post-money.

Liquidation Preference

A clause guaranteeing investors get their money back (often 1x, sometimes more) before common shareholders (founders/employees) see anything in an exit or shutdown — protects downside for the investor.

Pro-rata Rights

The right (not obligation) for an existing investor to invest more in a future round to maintain their ownership percentage — how a fund "doubles down" on its winners, as Murali's own bio references.

Vesting & Cliff

Founders/employees earn their equity or ESOP grants gradually over time (commonly 4 years), often with a 1-year "cliff" before any of it vests — protects the company if someone leaves early.

ESOP

Employee Stock Option Pool — equity set aside (typically 10–15% of the cap table) to grant to employees as compensation and retention incentive.

SAFE / Convertible Note

A simple early-stage instrument that isn't priced equity yet — it converts into shares at a future round, usually at a discount or capped valuation. Faster and cheaper to execute than a priced round.

Bridge Round

A smaller, often quick round raised between two larger priced rounds — usually to extend runway until the next major raise or a specific milestone.

Down Round

A funding round priced at a lower valuation than the company's previous round — dilutive and reputationally difficult, generally a sign of a struggling growth story or a broader market reset.

Board Seat vs. Observer Rights

A board seat gives an investor a formal vote on major company decisions; observer rights let them attend and hear board discussions without a vote — a lighter-touch form of oversight.

Drag-Along / Tag-Along Rights

Drag-along: majority shareholders can force minority holders to join a sale on the same terms. Tag-along: minority holders can insist on joining a sale the majority negotiates, on the same terms.

Anti-Dilution Protection

Adjusts an earlier investor's ownership or conversion price if a later round is priced lower (a down round), softening the dilution they'd otherwise suffer.

AIF (Category I / II / III)

Alternative Investment Fund — the SEBI-regulated structure Indian VC/PE funds like Inflexor operate under. Category I covers funds with positive economic/social impact (venture capital, SME, infrastructure, social-venture funds) — the category Inflexor's Technology Fund and Opportunities Fund are both registered under.

GP / LP

GP (General Partner) = the fund manager making investment decisions (Inflexor's partners). LP (Limited Partner) = the outside investors (institutions, family offices, HNIs) who commit capital to the fund and don't make day-to-day calls.

Carry & Management Fee

Management fee: an annual fee (commonly ~2% of committed capital) paid to the GP to run the fund. Carry (carried interest): the GP's share (commonly ~20%) of the fund's profits above a return threshold — the real upside-alignment mechanism for a fund manager.

IRR / MOIC / TVPI / DPI

IRR = annualized rate of return. MOIC = multiple on invested capital (total value ÷ capital invested). TVPI = total value to paid-in capital (similar to MOIC, fund-level). DPI = distributions to paid-in — the cash actually returned to LPs, as opposed to paper gains.

Follow-on Investment

Additional capital an existing investor puts into a company in a later round — distinct from pro-rata rights, which is the specific right to do so proportionally.

Due Diligence

The investigation process before finalizing an investment — financial, legal, technical (tech-IP diligence, in Inflexor's case), and reference checks on the founding team.

Series Nomenclature (Seed → A → B...)

Rough stage markers, not strict rules: Seed = earliest institutional money, often pre- or early-revenue. Series A = proven initial traction, scaling go-to-market. Series B+ = scaling a working model, often with clearer unit economics. Inflexor's typical entry point is pre-Series A to Series A.

What Satellites and Ceiling Fans Have in Common with Inflexor Full text read
Jatin Desai · April 2026
  • First public write-up of the S.E.T. (Science, Engineering, Technology) framework in Jatin's own words
  • Frames SET investing as a "meaningful departure from the traditional software or consumer-internet playbook" — requires evaluating technical moat, commercialization readiness, and tolerance for long gestation periods
The Founding Trinity
  • The Scientist — "Scientific Sovereignty": the IP anchor, a deep domain expert ensuring a genuine proprietary moat, not a thin veneer over existing models
  • The Engineer — the executioner: builds hardware-native stacks or complex software robustly and cost-effectively, without burning Silicon-Valley-scale capital ("Asset-Right")
  • The Business Strategist — the navigator: translates complex science into a compelling, must-have ROI, owns the journey from lab prototype to commercial product
  • Rare to find all three in one founder — Inflexor looks for the trinity distributed across 2–4 co-founders/senior leaders instead
The SET Filter (3 parts)
  • Technical Defensibility: hard-to-copy IP — if an incumbent could replicate it in 3–6 months with a few engineers, it's not SET
  • The India Arbitrage: cost-efficient R&D (global-standard product at a fraction of Silicon Valley burn) + talent density as real, structural moats
  • Outcome-Based Thinking: backing founders who sell outcomes ("guaranteed warehouse throughput"), not tools ("robotics software") — seat-based licensing is fading
Three founder red flags

The Bet With a Positive Expected Value That Will Bankrupt You Full text read
Pratik Ahuja · May 2026
  • Opens with a coin-flip thought experiment: +50%/-40% on a fair coin has positive expected value (+5%/flip) but bankrupts almost everyone who plays it repeatedly — the core illustration of non-ergodicity
  • Non-ergodicity: a process is ergodic if the average across many parallel "yous" equals one "you's" average over time. For multiplicative processes (like VC returns), these two diverge sharply
  • Expected value is an ensemble calculation ("if 1,000 parallel mes made this bet, what's the average?") — not the question you actually face, since you get one path through time, not 1,000
  • Losing 40% requires a 67% gain just to break even — asymmetry compounds with every step in multiplicative betting
  • LP, GP, and founder are each solving a different non-ergodicity problem at the same table: LP is closest to ergodic (diversified across GPs/vintages, can absorb J-curves); GP is diversified within a fund but serially exposed across fund generations; founder is maximally non-ergodic — one company, one path, no averaging
  • Example tension: a GP's "raise a risky bridge" logic (EV-positive if 1-in-5 shots works across the portfolio) versus the founder's logic (30% chance of losing the company they built) — "neither is wrong, they're playing different games"
  • Term sheets reframed as "ergodicity documents" — every clause redistributes non-ergodic risk between parties (GPs offload downside via preferences/anti-dilution while retaining upside via pro-rata/participation rights)
  • Reserve allocation (follow-on decisions) is the most consequential ongoing ergodicity decision a GP makes — a company that's de-risked (found PMF, strong cohorts) has a higher geometric growth rate, so the Kelly-rational move is to increase the stake (the basis for fighting for pro-rata rights)
  • Averaging down into a struggling company (the "sunk-cost follow-on") is called out as an ergodicity error — it reduces, not improves, the fund's compounding
  • References the Kelly Criterion and the common "never put more than 8–10% of the fund in one name" rule as a real mathematical approximation, not just folk wisdom
  • Closing reframe: instead of asking "what's the downside scenario," ask "what does this bet do to the path I'm actually on?"
